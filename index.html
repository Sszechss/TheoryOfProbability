Part 1 of 6

01Real-world problem

In this data, roughly one student in three failed math. The usual advice is to study more, and many families also pay for a tutor. One costs time and the other costs money, so I wanted to know whether the students who do these things actually pass more often.

Part 3 of 6

03Data collection

The data is the Student Performance dataset from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, file student-mat.csv. P. Cortez and A. Silva collected it in 2005–2006 at two public secondary schools in Portugal, using school reports and a questionnaire the students filled in. Each of the 395 rows is one student, with no names attached. Grades run from 0 to 20, and 10 is a pass.

The four columns I use (the file has 33)

How clean is it?

  • Nothing is missing. Every student has a value in every column.

4.1Experiment and sample space

The experiment: pick one student from the list at random, with every student equally likely.

S = {s1, s2, …, s395},  N(S) = 395

Most of the time I only care about two things: does the student study more than 5 hours, and did they pass? Recording just that gives a much smaller sample space with four outcomes.

S′ = {A ∩ Y,  A ∩ Yc,  Ac ∩ Y,  Ac ∩ Yc}

4.2Events and operations

Events are just sets of students, so the usual set operations apply. Here is how A and Y overlap.

Operations on A and Y (Definitions 2.2.1–2.2.3)
OperationMeaningStudents
A ∩ Ystudies a lot and passed69
A ∪ Yat least one of the two23 + 69 + 196 = 288
Acstudies 5 h or less395 − 92 = 303
(A ∪ Y)cneither395 − 288 = 107

A and Y are not mutually exclusive, since 69 students are in both. De Morgan's law checks out:

(A ∪ Y)c = Ac ∩ Yc  →  107 = 107 ✓

Students outside A ∪ Y are exactly the ones who study 5 h or less and did not pass.

For a mutually exclusive example, take the four answers to the study-time question: B1 is under 2 h, B2 is 2–5 h, B3 is 5–10 h and B4 is over 10 h. Each student picked one answer, so the groups can't overlap, and together they cover everyone.

Bi ∩ Bj = ∅ for i ≠ j,  and  105 + 198 + 65 + 27 = 395 ✓

A itself is B3 ∪ B4, which is why 65 + 27 = 92.

4.3Kolmogorov's axioms

Every student has probability 1/395, so any event has P(E) = N(E) / 395. Here is how the three axioms look on real numbers.

  1. P(E) ≥ 0. A count can't be negative, so a probability can't be either.
  2. P(S) = 1. That's 395/395. The four cells of S′ add up to one as well:

    0.175 + 0.058 + 0.496 + 0.271 = 1.000 (100%) ✓

  3. If E ∩ F = ∅, then P(E ∪ F) = P(E) + P(F). A student can't be in both A ∩ Y and Ac ∩ Y, and together these two make up Y:

    P(Y) = 0.175 + 0.496 = 0.671 (67%), and 69 + 196 = 265 ✓

4.4Classical probability and theorems

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability comes down to counting.

P(E) = N(E) / N(S)

P(Y) = 265/395 = 0.671 (67%)
P(A) = 92/395 = 0.233 (23%)
P(T) = 181/395 = 0.458 (46%)

So a randomly picked student passed with probability 0.671, about two times out of three.

Three theorems that follow from the axioms, checked two ways

2.3.1P(Yc) = 1 − P(Y)1 − 0.671 = 0.329 (33%)130/395 = 0.329 ✓
2.3.3A ∩ Y ⊂ Y ⇒ P(A ∩ Y) ≤ P(Y)0.175 ≤ 0.67169 ≤ 265 ✓

4.5Comparing shares

Is passing more common in some groups than in others? For each group I divide how many passed by how many students are in it. Strictly speaking that's a conditional probability, which belongs to Section 2.4, so here I keep it as a plain ratio.

Part 5 of 6

05Interpretation

  • Students in A pass about 10 percentage points more often than students outside it. For paid classes the gap is about 9 points.
  • The gap is modest. Almost all of it appears between 2–5 h and 5–10 h, and going past 10 hours adds nothing on top. Even students who study under 2 hours pass about two times out of three.
  • These numbers show that the events tend to happen together. They can't say why. Extra classes might help, or parents might pay for them because their child is already struggling, and this data can't tell those apart.
  • Study time is self-reported, and every student comes from two Portuguese schools in a single year, so I wouldn't stretch the result much beyond that.

Part 6 of 6

06Conclusion

Short answer: yes, a little.

In these 395 students, studying more than 5 hours a week and having paid classes both come with a higher pass rate, by roughly 10 percentage points. Most students pass either way, and the data can't show cause and effect. The axioms and Theorems 2.3.1, 2.3.3 and 2.3.6 all came out exactly right when I checked them by counting.

Appendix: the code

Show the Python code (pandas) 
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("student-mat.csv", sep=";")   # the UCI file uses ";"
n = len(df)                                    # 395

Y = df.G3 >= 10            # passed
A = df.studytime >= 3      # studies more than 5 hours a week
T = df.paid == "yes"       # paid extra classes
N = lambda e: int(e.sum())

print(N(Y), N(A), N(T))                            # 265 92 181
print(N(A & Y), N(A & ~Y), N(~A & Y), N(~A & ~Y))  # 69 23 196 107
print(N(A | Y), N(~(A | Y)))                       # 288 107 (De Morgan)
print(N(T & Y))                                   # 130
print(df.groupby("studytime").G3.agg(["size", lambda g: (g >= 10).sum()]))

for name, e in [("A", A), ("not A", ~A), ("T", T), ("not T", ~T)]:
    print(name, round(N(e & Y) / N(e), 3))          # share passed
